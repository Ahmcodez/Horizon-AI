Full retirement age, by birth year

Full retirement age (FRA) is the single number that every other Social Security calculation is measured against — your reduction for claiming early, your credit for waiting, and the baseline for spousal and survivor benefits. Here's yours.

If you were born in 1960 or later — which now covers the large majority of people still working toward retirement — your full retirement age is 67, and it isn't scheduled to move again under current law.

What full retirement age actually controls

FRA isn't when you're required to claim — it's the age at which you receive exactly 100% of your calculated benefit, no reduction and no bonus. Claim before it and your check is permanently reduced; claim after it, up to 70, and you pick up delayed retirement credits worth roughly 8% a year instead.

It's also the reference point for a spousal benefit (up to 50% of your FRA amount, not your actual reduced or increased benefit) and for whether the earnings test applies if you're still working while collecting.

FRA is not the same as Medicare eligibility

A common mix-up: Medicare eligibility starts at 65 for most people, regardless of your Social Security full retirement age. The two systems were never aligned to move together, which is why someone with an FRA of 67 still needs to handle Medicare enrollment two years earlier, separately from any Social Security claiming decision.

The earnings test disappears at FRA

If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, Social Security withholds part of your benefit once you earn above an annual limit. That withholding isn't a permanent loss — it's repaid gradually through a higher benefit later — but it trips people up every year regardless. Once you reach FRA, the earnings test simply stops applying, and you can earn any amount without any reduction.
