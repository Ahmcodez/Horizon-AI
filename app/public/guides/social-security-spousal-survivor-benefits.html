Social Security spousal and survivor benefits, explained

Two of the most misunderstood numbers in the whole system — and two of the most expensive to get wrong, since a bad claiming decision here doesn't just cost you, it can cost your spouse for the rest of their life too.

Social Security isn't only a benefit you earn from your own work record. If you're married, divorced after a long marriage, or widowed, you may be entitled to a benefit based on someone else's record instead — and in a lot of households, that second number ends up mattering more than the primary earner's own claiming age.

Spousal benefits: up to half, with conditions

A spousal benefit can pay you up to 50% of your spouse's benefit at their full retirement age (FRA) — not 50% of whatever they're actually collecting. If they claimed early and took a reduced check, your spousal benefit is still based on their full, unreduced FRA amount.

The catch: that 50% only applies if you claim your spousal benefit at your own FRA. File earlier and your share shrinks too, down to roughly 32.5% at 62. And you don't stack it on top of your own retirement benefit — Social Security pays whichever is larger, your own record or the spousal amount, not both added together.

Survivor benefits: up to the full amount

A survivor benefit works differently, and it's worth far more. A widow or widower at their own full retirement age can step into up to 100% of what their spouse was actually collecting when they died (or would have collected, if they hadn't yet claimed). Claim the survivor benefit early — as soon as age 60 — and it's reduced, down to about 71.5% at the earliest age.

This is exactly why the higher earner's claiming age matters for the whole household, not just themselves. If the higher earner claims at 62 and locks in a permanently reduced benefit, that lower number is also the ceiling on what their spouse can inherit as a survivor benefit later. Waiting until 70 doesn't just grow the higher earner's own check — it raises the floor under their spouse's eventual survivor benefit too.

Divorced? You may still qualify

If your marriage lasted at least 10 years and you're currently unmarried, you can claim a spousal benefit on an ex-spouse's record — even if they've remarried. It doesn't reduce what they or their current spouse receive, and in most cases they won't even be notified that you've filed. The same 10-year rule applies to divorced survivor benefits if your ex-spouse has since died.

The mistake that costs households the most

Treating each spouse's claiming decision as independent. The two ages interact — one spouse's record sets a ceiling on what the other can later receive as a spousal or survivor benefit — so the optimal strategy for a couple is rarely "both claim at the same age" or "both claim as soon as possible." It's a household calculation, run against both actual earnings records, not a per-person guess.
