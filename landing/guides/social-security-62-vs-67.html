Social Security at 62 vs. 67: what the wait actually costs you

Six years, one decision, and a difference that follows you for the rest of your life. Here's the actual math behind claiming at 62 versus waiting until full retirement age — not the rule of thumb, the real numbers.

If you were born in 1960 or later, your full retirement age (FRA) is 67. Claim at 62 — the earliest age Social Security allows — and your monthly check is reduced by 30% compared to what you'd get by waiting. That reduction isn't temporary. It doesn't taper off. It's the number you collect for the rest of your life, adjusted for inflation from that lower base.

Put in dollars: a benefit that would be $2,000 a month at 67 comes out to $1,400 a month at 62. Wait the extra five years and you're collecting $600 more every single month, for as long as you live.

Where the 30% actually comes from

Social Security reduces your benefit by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months you claim before full retirement age, then 5/12 of 1% for every month beyond that. Sixty months early (62 to 67) works out to exactly 30%. It's not a round number someone picked — it's what the formula produces.

The breakeven age, in plain terms

Claim early and you collect more total checks over more years. Claim later and each check is bigger. At some age, the two paths cross — the extra years of smaller checks stop outrunning the fewer years of bigger ones. For most people, that crossover lands somewhere in the late 70s, but it moves depending on your exact benefit amount, and it's the one number in this decision that's actually worth calculating precisely rather than estimating.

When claiming at 62 makes sense

You need the income now and don't have another bridge to retirement. Your health or family history points to a shorter-than-average retirement. You've stopped working entirely, so there's no earnings-test reduction eating into an early claim. Or you're single with no spouse depending on your record for a survivor benefit later.

When waiting until 67 makes sense

You're still working and would lose a chunk of an early benefit to the earnings test anyway. You're in good health with longevity in the family. You're the higher earner in a couple, since your claiming age also sets the floor for what your spouse could receive as a survivor benefit after you're gone. Or you simply have other income covering the gap and don't need the money at 62.

The number that actually matters is yours, not the average

Every figure above is the general SSA formula — the same for everyone. What it doesn't tell you is your breakeven age, your actual dollar difference, or how a spousal or survivor benefit changes the math for your household. That's the part worth running with your real numbers instead of a rule of thumb.
