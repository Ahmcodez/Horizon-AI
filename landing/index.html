The core decision

When should you claim Social Security?

There's no single right age — the SSA lets you claim any time between 62 and 70, and the amount changes permanently depending on when you do. Claiming at 62 locks in a reduced check for life; waiting until 70 locks in the largest one the SSA will pay you. The right age for you depends on a handful of factors most calculators never ask about.

Because the decision is permanent, it's worth treating like the six-figure decision it usually is rather than a form to get through quickly. A three-year difference in claiming age can move your lifetime income by well over $100,000 depending on how long you live and what else you have coming in.

MyClaimAge runs your actual earnings record — not a national average — against every age from 62 to 70, so you can see your own numbers before you decide. See the full 62 vs. 67 vs. 70 breakdown for the exact math, or jump to the comparison below.

  • Your health and family longevity. A longer expected lifespan generally favors waiting; a shorter one generally favors claiming earlier.
  • Other income and savings. If you need the income at 62, waiting may not be realistic regardless of the math.
  • Marital status. Spousal and survivor benefits depend on both partners' claiming ages, not just your own.
  • Whether you're still working. Earning above the annual limit before full retirement age temporarily withholds part of your benefit.
  • Your breakeven age. The age where a later, larger check catches up to an earlier, smaller one that's been paid longer.

62 vs. 67 vs. 70

Three ages, three very different checks.

Illustrative numbers for someone with a $2,690/mo benefit at full retirement age (67). Your own numbers will differ — this is the shape of the tradeoff, not a prediction.

62

$1,890/mo

The earliest claiming age. Reduces your benefit by up to 30% compared to full retirement age, permanently. Often the right call if you need the income now or don't expect a long retirement.

67

$2,690/mo

Full retirement age for anyone born in 1960 or later. No reduction and no delayed credit — this is your Primary Insurance Amount, the baseline every other age is measured against.

70

$3,320/mo

The last age delayed retirement credits accrue — roughly 8% per year past full retirement age. The largest possible check, if you can afford to wait and expect to need income for a long retirement.

The part most calculators skip

Taxes and Medicare change what actually lands in your account.

Your gross benefit and your net deposit are two different numbers. MyClaimAge shows both.

Federal (and sometimes state) taxes

Depending on your combined income from all sources, up to 85% of your Social Security benefit can be subject to federal income tax. A handful of states tax it too, on top of federal. MyClaimAge factors your likely tax exposure into the comparison, rather than only showing the gross benefit.

Medicare Part B and IRMAA

Once you're enrolled, your Medicare Part B premium — $202.90/mo at the standard 2026 rate — is deducted automatically from your check. Higher earners pay an additional IRMAA surcharge on top. MyClaimAge shows your net deposit after Medicare, so the number you see is the one you'll actually receive.

Common questions

Social Security claiming — answered plainly.

There is no single best age for everyone — it depends on your health, other income, and household situation. Claiming at 62 reduces your monthly benefit permanently, while waiting until 70 increases it. MyClaimAge models your specific numbers across every age from 62 to 70 so you can compare the real dollar difference for your situation. See the full 62 vs. 67 breakdown for the exact math.

Delaying benefits past full retirement age adds delayed retirement credits worth roughly 8% per year, up until age 70. Claiming before full retirement age instead reduces your benefit, by as much as 30% at age 62.

For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67 — the final step of a phase-in that began with 1983 legislation.

If you claim before full retirement age and continue working, Social Security withholds a portion of your benefit above an annual earnings limit. The limit and withholding rules change each year and no longer apply once you reach full retirement age.

It can be. Depending on your combined income from all sources, up to 85% of your benefit may be subject to federal income tax, and a handful of states tax it too. MyClaimAge factors this into the net numbers it shows you, rather than just the gross benefit amount.

Once you're enrolled, your Medicare Part B premium is deducted automatically from your Social Security payment, and higher earners pay an additional IRMAA surcharge. MyClaimAge shows your net deposit after Medicare, not just the pre-deduction benefit amount.
